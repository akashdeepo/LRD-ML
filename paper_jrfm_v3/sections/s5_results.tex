\section{Results}\label{sec:results}

\subsection{Implied volatility accounts for the gains over HAR}

Table~\ref{tab:ladder} compares each model with HAR. Adding the VIX and MOVE
lowers the pooled mean squared error by 3.1\% at one day and 5.5\% at one
week (Diebold--Mariano statistics of 5.23 and 5.35), and by 1.8\% at one
month, which is not significant. The gains are broad: HAR-X improves on HAR
for 111 of the 115 stocks at the weekly horizon.

\input{tables/t4_ladder}

Added to HAR without implied volatility, the persistence blocks do not
improve forecasts. Own-stock persistence worsens them significantly at the
daily and monthly horizons, and the effects of the cross-sectional and
sector states are not significant at the 5\% level. The only specifications
that improve on HAR, $A_5$ and $C$, are those that contain the VIX and MOVE.
Their gains are close to those of HAR-X at the weekly horizon (5.6\% and
5.8\%, against 5.5\%) and smaller at the daily horizon (2.9\% and 2.1\%,
against 3.1\%); at one month model $C$ is worse than HAR. The stock-level
results point the same way: model $C$ improves on HAR for 107 stocks at the
weekly horizon but for only 48 at the monthly horizon.

\subsection{Persistence beyond HAR-X}

Table~\ref{tab:harx} compares the persistence specifications directly with
HAR-X. The three registered extensions change the mean squared error by
between $-2.0\%$ and $+0.003\%$. None has a significant Diebold--Mariano
statistic in its favor, and at one month one is worse at the 5\% level and
another at the 10\% level. Model $C$ gains 0.26\% at one week ($t=0.41$) and
is significantly worse at one day ($-1.0\%$, $t=-2.14$) and at one month
($-3.4\%$, $t=-2.69$).

\input{tables/t5_harx}

The Clark--West statistics appear to point the other way. They are large and
positive for every specification at one day and one week, and the
Holm-adjusted $p$-values are below 0.05 for all three registered extensions
at those horizons. Two specifications therefore formally meet the registered
rule: the cross-sectional extension at one week, with a gain of 0.003\%, and
model $C$ at one week, with a gain of 0.26\%. Neither gain is economically
meaningful, and neither meets the 0.5\% threshold that we added after seeing
the results.

The two tests answer different questions, and the Giacomini--White test
resolves the apparent conflict. The Clark--West test asks whether the
persistence coefficients are zero in population; its adjustment removes the
noise that estimating these coefficients adds to the forecasts of the larger
model. A rejection therefore indicates that the persistence terms have
predictive content once HAR-X is controlled for. The Giacomini--White test
asks whether the estimated model forecasts better, conditional on recent
relative performance. It never favors a persistence specification: its
$p$-values for the registered extensions lie between 0.11 and 0.92, and its
only rejection, for model $C$ at one month ($p=0.018$), goes against the
persistence model, as does the near-rejection at one day ($p=0.064$). The
predictive content of the persistence terms is genuine but small, and
estimating the additional coefficients stock by stock (11 of them for model
$C$) costs more accuracy than it adds.

Figure~\ref{fig:cumulative} shows when the gains and losses accrue. The gain
of HAR-X over HAR accumulates steadily at the daily and weekly horizons, with
jumps in March 2020. The gain of model $C$ over HAR-X declines steadily at the
daily and monthly horizons and stays close to zero at the weekly horizon,
where its small positive balance appears only in 2025.

\subsection{Information timing}

The comparison between HAR and HAR-X is sensitive to a detail that is easily
overlooked. If the HAR terms are built from variance through day $t-1$, for
example by lagging all regressors by one day to avoid look-ahead bias, while
the VIX is the close of day $t$, the implied-volatility model receives one
more day of information than the benchmark. Table~\ref{tab:timing}
re-estimates both models with HAR inputs ending at $t-1$ and compares them
with the aligned specifications used elsewhere in the paper.

\input{tables/t6_timing}

Aligning the inputs lowers the error of HAR by 8.1\% at one day, 7.0\% at one
week and 2.9\% at one month, all significantly. Because HAR-X already
contains the VIX of day $t$, it gains less from the alignment, and its
advantage over HAR falls from 5.1\% to 3.1\%, from 7.8\% to 5.5\% and from
2.9\% to 1.8\%. Between 29\% and 39\% of the apparent value of implied
volatility is therefore a timing artifact. Earlier versions of this study
used the misaligned convention and reported the larger gains. Studies that
compare HAR with implied-volatility regressors should verify the same
alignment.

\subsection{Pooled, term-structure and market-level designs}

The per-stock design invites three objections, and Table~\ref{tab:designs}
addresses each with a design registered before its results were computed.

\input{tables/t7_designs}

First, per-stock regressions estimate many coefficients from 430 to about
1070 weekly observations, while the persistence state is common to all
stocks. A pooled regression with stock fixed effects estimates each
coefficient once from the whole panel. Pooling helps: model $C$ now improves
on pooled HAR-X by 0.40\% at one day and 0.34\% at one week, and the
Clark--West rejections survive the Holm adjustment. The gains are, however,
below the 0.5\% threshold and not significant by the Diebold--Mariano test,
and at one month the sign reverses.

Second, persistence might describe how long volatility stays high rather
than how high it is. We therefore forecast the slope of the variance term
structure, defined as the log ratio of mean variance over the next 22 days
to mean variance over the next 5. HAR improves on an expanding-mean
benchmark for this target by 3.3\%, so the slope is forecastable, but adding
persistence to HAR-X makes the forecasts worse, significantly so for model
$C$ ($-1.8\%$, $t=-3.92$).

Third, the state is a market-wide quantity and may matter only at the market
level. For a single series, the cross-sectional mean of the stock targets,
the persistence state improves on a market HAR-X by 3.4\% at one day and 2.0\%
at one week, with Diebold--Mariano statistics of 1.77 and 1.45. These are
the largest gains in the study, but with one series and 646 origins they are
not significant. An exploratory analysis in Appendix~\ref{app:market}
suggests that they reflect the slowly decaying variance risk premium after
crises rather than persistence.

\subsection{Calm and stressed periods}

Splitting the evaluation period by the quartiles of the VIX on the forecast
dates reveals no regime in which persistence improves forecasts
significantly. In the highest VIX quartile model $C$ improves on HAR-X by 2.4
percentage points at one week, with a Diebold--Mariano statistic of 1.36; in
the lowest quartile it is worse at every horizon; from March to December 2020
it is 0.2 points worse at one week and 5.6 points worse at one month. With
162 dates per quartile and 43 in 2020, these comparisons are descriptive.
