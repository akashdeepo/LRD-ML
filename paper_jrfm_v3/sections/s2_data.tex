\section{Data and Measurement}\label{sec:data}

\subsection{Sample}

Daily open, high, low and close prices for S\&P~500 constituents were
obtained from Bloomberg. The panel retains the 115 stocks observed on at
least 70\% of the 6136 trading days between 29 November 2001 and 21 April
2026; it covers all eleven GICS sectors. Membership is that of the April
2026 index, so firms that left the index earlier are excluded;
Section~\ref{sec:discussion} discusses the implications. Returns are daily
log price returns. For use as predictors they are winsorized point-in-time
at the expanding 0.1\% and 99.9\% quantiles after a 500-day warm-up, so that
no observation is trimmed with information from later dates. One special
dividend that appears as a price drop (Keurig Dr Pepper, 10 July 2018) is
added back. The market variables are the daily closes of the Cboe VIX index
and of the ICE BofA MOVE index of Treasury implied volatility; the
three-month Treasury bill rate is used in the portfolio exercise.

\subsection{Variance}

Daily variance is measured by the range estimator of
\citet{parkinson1980extreme},
\begin{equation}
\sigma^2_{i,t} = \frac{\left(\ln H_{i,t} - \ln L_{i,t}\right)^2}{4\ln 2},
\label{eq:parkinson}
\end{equation}
where $H_{i,t}$ and $L_{i,t}$ are the day's high and low prices. The
estimator uses intraday information, is several times more efficient than
the squared close-to-close return, and is available for the whole sample,
which five-minute realized variance is not. Days with a zero range are
treated as missing. All models use the logarithm of variance, which is close
to Gaussian and makes forecast errors comparable across stocks.

\subsection{Timing}

The forecast origin is the close of trading day $t$, and every predictor
uses only information available at that time, including the day's range and
return and the closing levels of the VIX and MOVE indices. The VIX is
computed until 4:15 p.m.\ Eastern time, fifteen minutes after the equity
close; we treat the two as simultaneous. Section~\ref{sec:results} shows
that this alignment matters. The forecast target for horizon $h$ is
\begin{equation}
y_{i,t,h} = \ln\Bigl(\frac{1}{h}\sum_{k=1}^{h} \sigma^2_{i,t+k}\Bigr),
\qquad h \in \{1, 5, 22\},
\label{eq:target}
\end{equation}
the log of mean variance over the next day, week and month. Forecasts are
made every fifth trading day, which gives 1078 weekly origins from 22
November 2004, the first date at which a 750-day window is available, to 21
April 2026.

\subsection{Full-sample memory}

Table~\ref{tab:memory} reports full-sample estimates of the fractional
parameter $d$ obtained with the log-periodogram estimator of
\citet{geweke1983estimation} (GPH) and the local Whittle estimator of
\citet{robinson1995gaussian}, both with bandwidth $m=T^{0.65}$. Returns show
no evidence of long memory: the mean estimate is $-0.02$. Log variance is
strongly persistent: the mean GPH estimate is 0.54, the mean local Whittle
estimate is 0.52, and 84\% of stocks have a GPH estimate above 0.5, the
boundary of stationarity. These values are in line with the established
stylized facts \citep{andersen2003modeling}. A Hurst exponent of 0.06,
obtained from the scaling of increments of log variance, would indicate
volatility much rougher than Brownian motion
\citep{gatheral2018volatility}. Appendix~\ref{app:rough} shows that a
two-factor Markovian model without roughness, observed through the sampling
noise of the range estimator, produces the same value, so roughness is not
identified at the daily frequency \citep{cont2024rough}. We do not use
roughness further.

Estimates of $d$ above 0.5 for most stocks call for caution, since they
imply nonstationary log variance. Long memory and a slowly decaying
short-memory process with occasional large episodes are difficult to
distinguish in samples of this length \citep{diebold2001long,
granger2004occasional}. The rolling estimates in the next section make the
difference visible.
