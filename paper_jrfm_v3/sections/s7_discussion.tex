\section{Discussion}\label{sec:discussion}

\subsection{Why persistence does not forecast}

The two halves of the paper fit together. Section~\ref{sec:window} shows that
the rolling persistence state is, to a close approximation, a smoothed record
of the largest volatility episodes of the past two to four years. It rises
when such an episode enters the window and falls, on a date fixed by the
window length, when the episode leaves. Sections~\ref{sec:results} and
\ref{sec:robust} show that, once the VIX and MOVE are in the model, the state
adds no forecasting value in any design we tried. The first result explains
the second. Implied volatility is a forward-looking price that already
reflects recent turmoil, partly through a variance risk premium that rises
after crises and decays slowly \citep{bates2000post, bekaert2014vix,
andersen2015risk}. A backward-looking indicator of when the last crisis
occurred has little to add. The Clark--West rejections show that it adds
something in population; the Giacomini--White tests show that the something
is too small to survive estimation.

Nothing here says that volatility lacks long memory, or that the persistence
of volatility shocks never changes. The full-sample estimates of
Table~\ref{tab:memory} are large, and our short-memory null reproduces their
calm-period level, which is consistent with a long literature showing that
level shifts and long memory are hard to separate
\citep{diebold2001long, perron2010long, varneskov2018combining}. The narrower
claim is that the time variation in rolling estimates is not evidence of time
variation in persistence. It is what a fixed process produces when a few
large episodes move through a fixed window.

\subsection{Implications for applied work}

Three practices follow for work that uses rolling memory estimates, Hurst
exponents or similar windowed statistics. First, report results for several
window lengths and check whether turning points move with the window; a
change that is structural should not. Second, regress the windowed statistic
on the largest value of an observable stress measure inside the same window;
a high $R^2$ indicates window composition rather than dynamics. Third,
compare the series with a short-memory null into which the observed episodes
are injected, and with estimators and tests built to separate long memory
from level shifts \citep{qu2011test, hou2014modified}. These are the
standard tools of the fields that met the same problem earlier, from
hydrology \citep{klemes1974hurst} to early-warning indicators in ecology and
climate \citep{dakos2008slowing, ditlevsen2010tipping,
boettiger2012quantifying} and sliding-window connectivity in neuroscience
\citep{leonardi2015spurious}.

For forecasting studies, three lessons are more general than this
application. Benchmark and challenger must use the same information set,
down to the day: a one-day lag in the benchmark's inputs inflated the value
of implied volatility by about a third. When a nested comparison gives a
Clark--West rejection without a lower loss, a Giacomini--White test says
whether the estimated model is useful. And machine-learning comparisons are
informative only if every learner is tuned under the same protocol: with
default settings gradient boosting lost 12--21\% to HAR, against 0.2--8\% once
tuned.

\subsection{Limitations}

The panel consists of current S\&P~500 members with long histories. Firms
that left the index are missing, which removes some of the most volatile
histories; this affects the level of volatility and of the memory estimates
more than the window mechanism, which is a property of the estimator. The
variance proxy is the daily range, not intraday realized variance; its
sampling noise lowers the level of $\hat d$ and of the Hurst exponent but not
the dates at which episodes enter and leave the window. Returns are price
returns, which matters only for the portfolio exercise. The forecast
evaluation period, 2013 to 2026, contains one extreme episode, in 2020, and
several moderate ones; the window evidence also uses 2008. A market without a
liquid implied-volatility index is where persistence could have the most to
add, but there the largest recent realized variance is available directly and
would be the natural benchmark. Finally, the evidence on the window mechanism
is for memory in volatility; a general analysis across estimators, asset
classes and markets is left for future work.

\section{Conclusions}\label{sec:conclusions}

Rolling long-memory estimates of stock volatility rise sharply in crises, and
it is tempting to read them as a measure of how persistent the market has
become. On 115 S\&P~500 stocks over 2001--2026 they carry no forecasting
information beyond implied volatility, under every design we registered:
per-stock and pooled regressions, the level and the term structure of future
variance, a market-level series, linear models and tuned machine-learning
estimators, calm and stressed periods, and volatility-managed portfolios.
The reason is that the rolling persistence state records which extreme
episodes are inside its window. It jumps when an episode enters, falls on the
day the episode leaves, does so at dates that move one-for-one with the
window length, is reproduced by a short-memory process, and is explained to
83\% by the largest VIX inside the window. Researchers who use rolling memory
estimates as a state variable should check these signatures before
interpreting them, and forecasting studies that compare HAR with implied
volatility should align the information sets of the two, which here removes
a third of the apparent value of the VIX.
