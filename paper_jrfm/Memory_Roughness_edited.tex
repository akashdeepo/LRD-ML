\Title{Volatility Persistence as a Financial State Variable:
Memory, Roughness, and Forecasting Across Market Regimes}

\Author{Akash Deep $^{1}$, Nicholas Appiah $^{1,*}$ and Svetlozar T. Rachev $^{1}$}
\AuthorNames{Akash Deep, Nicholas Appiah and Svetlozar T. Rachev}

\address[1]{Department of Mathematics and Statistics, Texas Tech University,
Lubbock, TX 79409, USA}
\corres{Correspondence:  \href{mailto:niappiah@ttu.edu}{niappiah@ttu.edu}}

\abstract{
This paper asks whether volatility persistence carries forecasting
information as a shared financial state beyond what standard benchmarks
already contain. Using 115 equities drawn from the S\&P~500 over November
2001--April 2026, we estimate rolling long-memory (GPH and local-Whittle)
and roughness measures on Parkinson range-based variance and evaluate them
in a walk-forward, point-in-time design against HAR and HAR-X (HAR with VIX
and MOVE) benchmarks, using panel-aware Diebold--Mariano and Clark--West
tests. Volatility proxies display strong persistence and low Hurst
exponents, and the cross-sectional persistence state nearly doubles in the
2008 and 2020 crises and co-moves with the VIX. Its forecasting content,
however, is largely redundant with the market-state controls: the full
persistence specification improves on HAR by 4.5--8.3\% at daily and
weekly horizons but never significantly on HAR-X; pre-specified variants
that add persistence to HAR-X directly do not lower forecast error; pooled
estimation recovers only a negligible gain; and the persistence state
predicts neither the term-structure slope of future variance nor
market-level variance beyond VIX and MOVE. Volatility-managed portfolio
differences are not statistically significant. Persistence is a coherent
descriptive market state whose forecasting information is already
contained in implied volatility.}

realized volatility; persistence dynamics}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}
% =============================================================================

Volatility forecasting occupies a central role in financial econometrics
because volatility enters risk management, derivative pricing, portfolio
allocation, margin determination, stress testing, and macro-financial
monitoring. A large empirical literature has documented that volatility
measures exhibit substantially greater persistence than raw asset returns.
Classical long-memory models formalized this observation by extending
conventional weak-dependence frameworks to allow autocorrelations that
decay at hyperbolic rather than exponential rates
\citep{granger1980introduction,hosking1981fractional}. Subsequent work
incorporated fractional dependence into conditional-volatility specifications
through FIGARCH-type models \citep{baillie1996fractionally}, while the
realized-volatility literature improved the empirical measurement of latent
volatility dynamics using high-frequency data
\citep{andersen2003modeling,barndorff2002econometric}.

Despite extensive empirical evidence for persistence in volatility proxies,
the interpretation of persistence remains controversial. One branch of the
literature views long-memory behavior as reflecting structural
characteristics of financial markets, including heterogeneous trading
horizons, slow information diffusion, and institutional frictions
\citep{muller1997volatilities}. Another branch argues that apparent
long-range dependence may arise from structural breaks, stochastic regime
changes, or nonstationary volatility dynamics rather than genuine fractional
dependence \citep{mikosch2004nonstationarities}. As a result, the economic
interpretation of persistence measures remains unsettled. The central issue
is therefore not simply whether a fractional parameter can be estimated, but
whether persistence measures behave coherently across market states and
contain useful information for forecasting and risk management.

This paper examines volatility persistence from a reduced-form perspective.
Rather than interpreting the memory parameter as a purely technical object
associated with fractional differencing, we investigate whether rolling
persistence measures behave empirically like indicators of the temporal
propagation of uncertainty in financial markets. More specifically, we study
whether persistence measures rise during sustained stress episodes, whether
they co-move with broad market uncertainty indicators, and whether they
improve volatility forecasts beyond standard benchmark models. The analysis
does not attempt to identify a unique structural mechanism generating
persistence. Instead, persistence is treated as an empirical summary
statistic whose dynamics may reflect several overlapping mechanisms,
including heterogeneous investor horizons, liquidity conditions, delayed
information processing, regime shifts, and market-wide stress propagation.

The paper also considers the relationship between long-memory behavior and
rough volatility. Recent research has shown that volatility paths often
display substantial local irregularity, with estimated Hurst exponents
significantly below the Brownian benchmark of $\frac{1}{2}$
\citep{gatheral2018volatility,bennedsen2021decoupling}. Although roughness
and long memory are sometimes presented as competing explanations of
volatility dynamics, they describe different temporal characteristics. Long
memory concerns the persistence of dependence over long horizons, whereas
roughness concerns local path irregularity at very short horizons. The
empirical analysis in this paper suggests that both characteristics may
coexist within the same volatility process.

A further issue concerns the role of machine learning in volatility
forecasting. Flexible forecasting algorithms can capture nonlinear
interactions and high-dimensional predictor structures that may be difficult
to represent using conventional parametric models. However, purely
data-driven forecasting systems often lack economic interpretability and may
produce unstable results outside the estimation sample
\citep{gu2020empirical}. The present paper adopts an intermediate approach:
econometric methods are used to construct interpretable persistence-related
features, while forecasting systems incorporate these features alongside
conventional volatility predictors and market-state variables.

The empirical analysis uses a panel of 115 S\&P~500 stocks over the period
November 2001 through April 2026. We estimate rolling long-memory measures
using both the Geweke--Porter-Hudak and local-Whittle estimators and
estimate local roughness using rolling Hurst exponents. These variables are
incorporated into a forecasting framework that compares heterogeneous
autoregressive (HAR) models, HAR models with market-state controls (HAR-X),
persistence-augmented regressions, shrinkage estimators, and tree-based
machine-learning methods.

The central finding is negative in a specific and, we argue, useful
way. Own-stock persistence and roughness add nothing to HAR. The shared
persistence state, meaning cross-sectional and sectoral averages of rolling
memory estimates and their interactions with market stress, does improve on
HAR, but its improvement is almost entirely the improvement that VIX and
MOVE deliver on their own. Tested directly against HAR-X with the
appropriate nested-model statistics, no persistence specification lowers
forecast error at any horizon, and a set of follow-up designs fixed in
advance (adding persistence blocks to HAR-X one at a time, letting
persistence modulate the HAR weights, pooled estimation across stocks, a
term-structure target, and a market-level target) shows that the
information the persistence state carries is small and already contained
in implied volatility.

The paper makes three contributions. First, it documents persistent
low-frequency dependence alongside roughness diagnostics across a broad
equity panel, distinguishing these temporal characteristics without treating
either as proof of a particular stochastic law, and shows that a
cross-sectional persistence state is a coherent market variable that nearly
doubles in the global financial crisis and in COVID and co-moves with the
VIX. Second, it provides a complete, point-in-time forecasting evaluation of
persistence at the stock, cross-sectional, sectoral, and interaction levels
against both HAR and HAR-X, with panel-aware Diebold--Mariano and
Clark--West inference and pre-specified follow-up designs, and reports the
direct comparisons with HAR-X that work on persistence features typically
omits. Third, it relates this evidence to a volatility-managed portfolio
application with formal tests of Sharpe-ratio differences and compares
linear, shrinkage, and tree-based estimators on the same feature space. The
reported results favor structured linear forecasts in this setting, not a
general rejection of nonlinear methods.

The scope of the evidence is deliberately reduced-form. Persistence rises
during major stress episodes and co-moves with the VIX, but the lead--lag
diagnostic does not establish a leading stress signal, and the persistence
state does not forecast how slowly variance decays. Every specification is
tested against both HAR and HAR-X. The contribution is therefore a
characterization: shared persistence states are informative descriptions
of market conditions whose forecasting content, for stock-level variance,
is captured by implied-volatility indices.

The remainder of the paper proceeds as follows. Section~\ref{sec:literature}
reviews the literature. Section~\ref{sec:theory} discusses the theoretical
foundations of persistence. Section~\ref{sec:econometrics} presents the
econometric framework. Section~\ref{sec:structural} develops the
reduced-form interpretation. Section~\ref{sec:ml} introduces the forecasting
framework. Section~\ref{sec:data} describes the data and empirical design.
Section~\ref{sec:results} presents the results. Section~\ref{sec:economic}
discusses the economic implications. Section~\ref{sec:conclusion} concludes.

\section{Literature Review: Persistence, Roughness, and Volatility}
\label{sec:literature}
% =============================================================================

The literature on volatility persistence originates with foundational work
on fractional integration and ARFIMA processes, which extended classical
time-series analysis by allowing autocorrelation structures with
hyperbolically decaying dependence rather than conventional exponential
decay \citep{granger1980introduction,hosking1981fractional}. These
developments became especially important in financial econometrics because
volatility proxies such as squared returns, absolute returns, and realized
variance measures exhibit substantially stronger persistence than raw asset
returns themselves \citep{ding1993long}. The resulting evidence motivated
the development of models capable of capturing long-range dependence in
financial volatility while remaining consistent with broader conditional
heteroskedasticity frameworks.

One important contribution in this direction was the FIGARCH model
introduced by \citet{baillie1996fractionally}, which incorporated
fractional integration directly into conditional variance dynamics.
FIGARCH generalized conventional GARCH specifications by allowing shocks to
decay at hyperbolic rates, thereby accommodating the empirically observed
persistence of volatility. The model rapidly became a standard benchmark in
long-memory volatility research because it provided a tractable parametric
representation of persistent conditional heteroskedasticity. Subsequent
extensions introduced asymmetric responses, multivariate formulations, and
alternative fractional structures, further expanding the long-memory
volatility literature.

At approximately the same time, the realized-volatility literature
substantially improved the empirical measurement of latent volatility
processes. \citet{andersen2003modeling} and \citet{barndorff2002econometric}
demonstrated that high-frequency financial data could be used to construct
more accurate ex post measures of integrated volatility. These developments
strengthened empirical evidence for volatility persistence because
realized-volatility measures contain less measurement noise than traditional
squared-return proxies. The resulting data environment allowed researchers
to study persistence properties with considerably greater precision.

Another important development was the HAR model proposed by
\citet{corsi2009simple}. Rather than imposing exact fractional integration,
the HAR framework approximates long-memory behavior using a parsimonious
multi-horizon structure involving daily, weekly, and monthly
realized-volatility components. The HAR model proved empirically successful
because it combines forecasting accuracy with economic interpretability. In
particular, it is consistent with the idea that financial markets contain
heterogeneous agents operating at different investment horizons
\citep{muller1997volatilities}. The HAR framework remains one of the most
widely used benchmarks in volatility forecasting.

Despite substantial empirical support for persistence, the interpretation of
persistence remains controversial. One branch of the literature views long
memory as reflecting genuine structural characteristics of financial markets,
including heterogeneous trading frequencies, delayed information diffusion,
and institutional frictions. Another branch argues that apparent long-range
dependence may arise from structural breaks, regime changes, stochastic
volatility shifts, or nonlinear dynamics rather than from genuine fractional
integration \citep{mikosch2004nonstationarities}. This critique implies that
estimated persistence parameters should be interpreted cautiously because
they may reflect evolving market conditions rather than stable stochastic
mechanisms.

The debate surrounding long memory became even more important following the
emergence of the rough-volatility literature. \citet{gatheral2018volatility}
showed that volatility paths frequently exhibit substantial local
irregularity, with estimated Hurst exponents significantly below the
Brownian-motion benchmark of $H=\frac{1}{2}$. Subsequent work clarified that
roughness and long memory are not necessarily competing concepts.
\citet{bennedsen2021decoupling} argued that short-run roughness and
long-horizon persistence may represent distinct temporal features of
volatility processes. The empirical analysis adopts
this perspective by estimating both rolling persistence measures and rolling
Hurst exponents within the same forecasting framework.

In parallel with these econometric developments, machine learning has become
increasingly important in volatility forecasting and empirical asset pricing.
Flexible learning algorithms are capable of capturing nonlinear interactions,
threshold effects, and high-dimensional predictor structures
\citep{breiman2001random,friedman2001greedy,gu2020empirical,hastie2009elements}.
However, purely data-driven approaches may produce unstable results outside
the estimation sample and frequently lack economic interpretability.

This tension between statistical flexibility and economic interpretation has
become a central issue in modern empirical finance. Recent research
increasingly emphasizes hybrid approaches in which economically motivated
features are combined with flexible forecasting architectures. In this
setting, the quality and interpretability of the feature space may be more
important than the complexity of the forecasting algorithm itself.

Taken together, this literature motivates a focused empirical question:
does estimating persistence add forecasting information beyond the
multi-horizon dynamics captured by HAR and the market conditions captured
by HAR-X? The present paper addresses this question by separating own-stock
features from cross-sectional aggregates, sectoral states, and
persistence--stress interactions. Its contribution is this comparative
assessment of persistence as a forecasting state variable, rather than a
new fractional model or identification of an information-diffusion mechanism.

\section{Theoretical Foundations of Long Memory and Information
Persistence}
\label{sec:theory}
% =============================================================================

The concept of long memory emerged from attempts to describe stochastic
processes whose dependence structures cannot be adequately represented by
conventional short-memory models. Long-memory processes retain measurable
dependence over substantially longer horizons, typically characterized
through a slowly decaying autocovariance structure in which dependence
declines at a hyperbolic rather than exponential rate
\citep{granger1980introduction,hosking1981fractional}.

Formally, a covariance-stationary long-memory process may be characterized
by an autocovariance function satisfying
\begin{equation}
  \gamma(k) \sim c_\gamma\, k^{2d-1}\quad \text{as } k\to\infty,
  \label{eq:lm-autocov}
\end{equation}
where $d\in(0,\frac{1}{2})$ denotes the memory parameter.

Because $2d-1>-1$,
the autocovariances are not absolutely summable, implying persistent
dependence across long horizons. Equivalent characterizations arise in the
frequency domain,
\begin{equation}
  f(\lambda) \sim c_f\, \lambda^{-2d}, \quad \lambda \to 0,
\end{equation}
providing the foundation for semiparametric estimators of long memory,
including the Geweke--Porter-Hudak and local-Whittle procedures employed
later in the paper \citep{robinson1995gaussian}.

The fractional-difference operator $(1-L)^d$ provides the bridge between
these probabilistic properties and empirically estimable time-series models.
Fractionally integrated ARFIMA systems express current observations as
weighted combinations of past innovations, where the weights decay gradually
rather than disappearing rapidly. Relative to standard ARMA structures,
fractional integration allows shocks to persist over much longer horizons
while preserving covariance stationarity when $d<\frac{1}{2}$. In financial
econometrics, this framework proved especially useful because volatility
measures routinely display persistence patterns that are inconsistent with
short-memory dynamics.

Although long-memory models originated as statistical descriptions of
temporal dependence, their interpretation in financial applications
naturally raises broader economic questions. When persistence is observed in
volatility proxies rather than in returns themselves, the memory parameter
may be viewed as summarizing the duration over which uncertainty shocks
continue to influence market behavior. Importantly, this interpretation
should not be understood as structural identification of a unique mechanism.
Rather, persistence measures are best viewed as reduced-form summaries
reflecting several overlapping economic forces that jointly influence the
temporal propagation of volatility.

Several economic mechanisms could generate persistent volatility.
Heterogeneous trading horizons can aggregate into slow adjustment
\citep{muller1997volatilities,corsi2009simple}; delayed information
processing, liquidity constraints, and gradual portfolio rebalancing may
also prolong responses to shocks. These channels motivate studying
persistence, but the empirical design does not distinguish among them.
Regime changes and structural breaks remain alternative explanations for
similar low-frequency behavior \citep{mikosch2004nonstationarities}.

These considerations suggest that persistence measures may serve as
reduced-form indicators of the temporal organization of uncertainty in
financial markets. A stylized representation may be written as
\begin{equation}
  I_t \;=\; \sum_{k=0}^{\infty} w_k\, \varepsilon_{t-k}, \qquad
  w_k \sim c\, k^{d-1},
  \label{eq:info-persistence}
\end{equation}
where $I_t$ denotes an uncertainty-related process influenced by past
informational shocks $\varepsilon_{t-k}$, and $d$ governs the speed at
which the influence of past shocks declines over time.

It is important, however, to distinguish carefully between statistical
persistence and economic interpretation. The presence of estimated long
memory does not necessarily imply that markets literally follow stationary
fractional stochastic laws. Structural breaks, regime changes, stochastic
volatility shifts, and evolving institutional environments may generate
empirical behavior resembling long-range dependence. Consequently,
persistence measures should not be interpreted mechanically as identifying
a single structural mechanism. Instead, they should be viewed as empirical
indicators whose behavior may reflect several interacting economic channels
simultaneously.

The interpretation developed in this paper therefore remains intentionally
reduced-form. The central empirical question is not whether a unique
structural mechanism can be identified, but rather whether persistence
measures behave coherently across market states and whether they contain
useful forecasting information beyond conventional volatility predictors.
If persistence measures rise systematically during sustained stress
episodes, co-move with broad uncertainty indicators, and improve volatility
forecasts during crisis periods, then they may provide economically
informative summaries of market conditions even without full structural
identification.

This perspective also accommodates the rough-volatility literature.
Roughness and long memory describe different aspects of temporal dependence
rather than mutually exclusive mechanisms. Long memory concerns
low-frequency dependence and the persistence of shocks across longer
horizons. Roughness concerns local path irregularity at very short horizons.
A rough-volatility process satisfies
\begin{equation}
  \mathrm{E}\bigl|\log\sigma^2_{t+\Delta} - \log\sigma^2_t\bigr|^q
  \;\propto\; \Delta^{qH},
  \label{eq:rough-scaling-sec3}
\end{equation}
where $H<\frac{1}{2}$ implies rougher behavior than standard Brownian motion
\citep{gatheral2018volatility}. A volatility process may therefore be
simultaneously rough at short horizons and persistent at longer horizons.
Financial markets may react sharply and irregularly immediately after news
arrivals while still exhibiting prolonged uncertainty propagation over
subsequent periods.

The coexistence of roughness and persistence suggests that volatility
dynamics should be analyzed across multiple temporal scales rather than
through a single dependence measure. High-frequency volatility fluctuations
may primarily reflect microstructure effects and rapid information arrivals,
whereas medium- and long-horizon persistence may reflect broader
institutional adjustment processes, funding conditions, and macro-financial
uncertainty propagation. The empirical framework developed later in the
paper incorporates both dimensions simultaneously through rolling estimates
of long-memory parameters and rolling Hurst exponents.

For forecasting, the relevant question is whether estimated persistence
summarizes dependence not already captured by volatility lags and
market-state controls. The empirical sections investigate this through
forecast comparisons across horizons and regimes. These comparisons assess
predictive usefulness, not the expected duration of an identified shock.

\section{Econometric Modeling of Fractional and Rough Volatility}
\label{sec:econometrics}
% =============================================================================

The econometric analysis of volatility persistence has evolved along two
closely related but conceptually distinct directions. The first focuses on
long-memory dynamics and emphasizes slow hyperbolic decay over medium and
long horizons. The second focuses on rough volatility and emphasizes highly
irregular local behavior at very short horizons. The long-memory literature
developed primarily within discrete-time econometrics through fractional
integration and ARFIMA-type models. Rough-volatility models, by contrast,
emerged from continuous-time stochastic-volatility theory and high-frequency
financial econometrics. Recent empirical evidence suggests that these two
perspectives are not mutually exclusive. The empirical framework developed
in this paper therefore incorporates both dimensions in a common forecasting
framework.

\subsection{ARFIMA, GARCH, and FIGARCH}

The ARFIMA framework provides a discrete-time representation of fractional
dependence. For a covariance-stationary process $\{X_t\}$,
\begin{equation}
  \phi(L)\, (1-L)^d\, X_t \;=\; \theta(L)\,\eta_t,
\end{equation}
where $\phi(L)$ and $\theta(L)$ are finite-order lag polynomials, $d$
denotes the fractional integration parameter, and $\eta_t$ is a white-noise
innovation process \citep{granger1980introduction,hosking1981fractional}.
The operator $(1-L)^d$ generalizes conventional integer differencing to
fractional orders and allows shocks to decay gradually over long horizons.
When applied to volatility proxies such as squared returns, absolute
returns, range-based volatility measures, or realized variance, ARFIMA
models provide parsimonious representations of long-range dependence in
financial volatility. Unlike standard short-memory models, fractional
integration allows dependence to persist over extended periods while
preserving covariance stationarity when $d<\frac{1}{2}$.

The conditional heteroskedasticity literature approached volatility
persistence from a different perspective. Beginning with the ARCH model of
\citet{engle1982autoregressive} and the GARCH extension of
\citet{bollerslev1986generalized}, volatility dynamics were modeled directly
through evolving conditional variance equations. Standard GARCH systems
successfully capture volatility clustering but imply geometric decay in
the effects of shocks. Empirical volatility series, however, often display
substantially slower decay than conventional GARCH structures permit. The
FIGARCH model of \citet{baillie1996fractionally} addressed this limitation
by embedding fractional integration directly within the conditional-variance
recursion,
\begin{equation}
  \bigl[1 - \beta(L)\bigr]\, h_t \;=\; \omega
  + \bigl[1 - \beta(L) - \phi(L)(1-L)^d\bigr]\, \varepsilon_t^2 \,,
\end{equation}
where $h_t$ denotes conditional variance and $\varepsilon_t$ denotes return
innovations. FIGARCH allows volatility shocks to decay hyperbolically rather
than exponentially while preserving the conditional-heteroskedasticity
structure.

The FIGARCH framework became particularly influential because it provided an
explicit connection between long-memory econometrics and volatility
modeling. Numerous subsequent extensions introduced asymmetric effects,
multivariate specifications, nonlinear dynamics, and regime dependence.
Nevertheless, the central empirical motivation remained unchanged:
volatility shocks appear to dissipate much more slowly than conventional
short-memory volatility models predict.

An important practical issue concerns interpretation. Although ARFIMA and
FIGARCH systems estimate similar persistence behavior, they represent
different modeling philosophies. ARFIMA-type systems emphasize the
dependence structure of the observed process itself, whereas GARCH-type
systems emphasize the evolution of latent conditional variance. In practice,
both approaches frequently generate evidence consistent with persistent
volatility dynamics.

\subsection{Realized Volatility and HAR Models}

The development of realized-volatility methodology substantially improved
empirical analysis of volatility persistence. Traditional volatility proxies
based on squared daily returns contain substantial measurement noise and
therefore obscure underlying dependence structures. High-frequency financial
data made it possible to construct more accurate ex post measures of
integrated volatility through realized-variance estimators
\citep{andersen2003modeling,barndorff2002econometric}.
Realized-volatility measures significantly strengthened empirical evidence
for persistent volatility dynamics because they provide less noisy
approximations to latent volatility processes. These measures also
facilitated more precise estimation of long-memory behavior and improved
volatility forecasting performance across a wide range of applications.

An especially important contribution was the heterogeneous autoregressive
(HAR) model proposed by \citet{corsi2009simple}. The HAR framework
approximates long-memory behavior using a parsimonious multi-horizon
structure rather than imposing exact fractional integration,
\begin{equation}
  \log \mathrm{RV}_{t+1} \;=\; \beta_0 + \beta_d \log \mathrm{RV}^{(d)}_t
  + \beta_w \log \mathrm{RV}^{(w)}_t
  + \beta_m \log \mathrm{RV}^{(m)}_t
  + u_{t+1},
\end{equation}
where $\mathrm{RV}^{(d)}_t$, $\mathrm{RV}^{(w)}_t$, and
$\mathrm{RV}^{(m)}_t$ denote daily, weekly, and monthly realized-volatility
components. The HAR model proved remarkably successful empirically because
it captures many observed features of long-memory volatility dynamics while
remaining computationally simple and economically interpretable. The
framework is closely connected to heterogeneous-agent theories in which
market participants operate at different investment horizons
\citep{muller1997volatilities}. Daily components capture short-horizon
trading activity, whereas weekly and monthly components reflect
slower-moving institutional and macroeconomic adjustments.

The HAR framework also became a dominant benchmark in volatility forecasting
because of its strong empirical performance relative to more complex
nonlinear specifications. In the present paper, HAR models serve as the
baseline forecasting architecture against which persistence-augmented models
are evaluated.

\subsection{Rough Volatility}

The rough-volatility literature introduced a complementary perspective on
volatility dynamics by emphasizing local path irregularity rather than
long-horizon persistence. \citet{gatheral2018volatility} showed that
volatility paths estimated from high-frequency financial data often display
substantially rougher behavior than implied by standard Brownian-motion
models. In particular, estimated Hurst exponents frequently lie well below
the classical benchmark $H=\frac{1}{2}$.

A simplified rough-volatility representation may be written as
\begin{equation}
  \log \sigma_t \;=\; \mu + \nu\, B^H_t,
\end{equation}
where $B^H_t$ denotes fractional Brownian motion with Hurst exponent
$H<\frac{1}{2}$. From an econometric standpoint, roughness is commonly
characterized through scaling relations involving moments of volatility
increments,
\begin{equation}
  \mathrm{E}|\log\sigma^2_{t+\Delta} - \log\sigma^2_t|^q \;\propto\; \Delta^{qH},
\end{equation}
where $H$ governs the scaling of local volatility fluctuations across
short horizons. Let $m(q,\Delta)$ denote the empirical $q$th absolute
moment of log-volatility increments at lag $\Delta$. We estimate $H_t$
as the slope from regressing $\log m(q,\Delta)$ on $\log\Delta$ over
a small grid of lags, divided by $q$.

The rough-volatility framework proved highly successful in explaining
several empirical features of financial markets, including short-horizon
volatility behavior and implied-volatility surface dynamics
\citep{bennedsen2021decoupling}. Rough models also provided improved
consistency with observed option-market data and generated substantial
interest in both academic and practitioner communities.

Importantly, roughness and long memory characterize different temporal
properties of volatility. Roughness concerns highly local short-term
irregularity, whereas long memory concerns persistence across broader
temporal horizons. A volatility process may therefore be simultaneously
rough and persistent. The empirical framework adopted in this paper
explicitly incorporates both dimensions through rolling estimates of
long-memory parameters and rolling Hurst exponents.

\subsection{Semiparametric Estimation of Long Memory}

Empirical estimation of long memory presents substantial econometric
challenges because dependence structures must be distinguished from
nonstationarity, regime shifts, and structural breaks. The literature
therefore developed several semiparametric estimation procedures that focus
specifically on low-frequency behavior.

One widely used approach is the Geweke--Porter-Hudak (GPH)
log-periodogram estimator \citep{geweke1983estimation}. The estimator
exploits the low-frequency behavior of the spectral density by regressing
the logarithm of the periodogram on transformed Fourier frequencies. Using
the first $m$ low-frequency ordinates, the estimator takes the form
\begin{equation}
  \log I(\lambda_j) \;=\; \alpha
  - d\,\log\bigl[4\sin^2(\lambda_j/2)\bigr] + u_j,
\end{equation}
where $I(\lambda_j)$ is the periodogram at Fourier frequency
$\lambda_j=2\pi j/T$, $j=1,\ldots,m$, and $T$ is the number of observations.
The bandwidth is $m=\lfloor T^{0.65}\rfloor$. If $\hat\beta$ is the estimated
slope on $\log[4\sin^2(\lambda_j/2)]$, the displayed regression implies
$\hat d=-\hat\beta$.

Another important procedure is the local-Whittle estimator proposed by
\citet{robinson1995gaussian}. Rather than relying on regression, the
local-Whittle approach minimizes an approximation to the Gaussian likelihood
over the low-frequency band of the spectrum. The estimator generally
exhibits lower asymptotic variance under broad conditions and has become one
of the standard semiparametric procedures in long-memory econometrics. Both
estimators focus specifically on low-frequency dependence without imposing
complete parametric ARMA or GARCH structures, making them attractive for
empirical applications in which the short-memory component may be
misspecified or unstable.

In addition to semiparametric methods, the present paper also uses FIGARCH
estimates on representative subsets of stocks as parametric cross-checks.
Combining semiparametric and parametric approaches helps reduce dependence
on any single modeling framework and improves robustness of the empirical
conclusions.

\subsection{Identification Challenges}

Several identification problems complicate empirical interpretation of
persistence estimates. The first concerns structural nonstationarity.
Repeated regime shifts, evolving volatility states, and structural breaks
can generate empirical behavior resembling fractional dependence even when
the underlying process is not truly fractionally integrated
\citep{mikosch2004nonstationarities}. As a result, estimated persistence
parameters should not automatically be interpreted as evidence for
stationary long-memory laws.

The second challenge concerns the distinction between highly persistent
GARCH systems and genuine fractional integration. Near-integrated GARCH
models may produce empirical dynamics that are difficult to distinguish from
FIGARCH processes in finite samples, especially when persistence parameters
approach unity. A third challenge concerns rough-volatility estimation.
High-frequency financial data contain substantial market microstructure
noise arising from bid--ask bounce, asynchronous trading, discreteness
effects, and liquidity frictions. Unless realized-volatility measures are
carefully constructed, estimated roughness parameters may partly reflect
measurement distortions rather than genuine volatility dynamics.

The present paper addresses these issues through several robustness
procedures. Persistence is estimated on the Parkinson range-based variance
proxy with both the GPH and local-Whittle estimators, FIGARCH estimates
provide a parametric cross-check, rolling estimation windows of varying
lengths are compared, and the forecasting target is alternatively defined
from squared returns. The objective is not to establish exact
structural identification of fractional stochastic laws, but rather to
determine whether persistence-related measures behave coherently across
market states and contain useful forecasting information.

These identification issues are central to the broader interpretation
developed in the next section, where persistence measures are viewed as
reduced-form indicators of uncertainty propagation rather than as
mechanically interpreted fractional coefficients.

\section{Persistence as a Reduced-Form Financial State Variable}
\label{sec:structural}
% =============================================================================

The memory parameter $d$ describes the decay of temporal dependence under a
fractional model. In the empirical application, its rolling estimate
$\hat d_{i,t}$ is used as a summary of recent dependence in a volatility
proxy. A financial state variable here means an observable or estimated
predictor summarizing market conditions; it does not imply a structurally
identified or sufficient state representation. Estimated persistence can
reflect fractional dependence, regime shifts, measurement effects, or
several mechanisms operating together.

The central hypothesis concerns the level of aggregation at which
persistence is informative. An own-stock estimate may largely duplicate
the information in that stock's daily, weekly, and monthly volatility
components. Cross-sectional and sectoral averages may summarize shared
dependence less visible in those individual histories. Interactions with
VIX and MOVE allow the association between persistence and future
volatility to vary with market conditions. The forecasting design assesses
these possibilities without assuming that each feature block must improve
accuracy.

Three empirical patterns would be consistent with this interpretation.
First, rolling persistence states may vary across calm and stressed
periods. Second, they may co-move with market-wide uncertainty indicators
while still contributing information conditional on those indicators.
Third, additional forecasting value may depend on the horizon and regime.
None of these patterns alone establishes a causal channel, and co-movement
with stress is not equivalent to forecasting its onset or length.

The duration and propagation of uncertainty provide an economic
interpretation rather than a directly estimated outcome. Heterogeneous
adjustment, liquidity constraints, and delayed information processing can
motivate slow dependence decay, as discussed in Section~\ref{sec:theory}.
However, this paper does not estimate shock half-lives, crisis exit
probabilities, or the expected time until volatility normalizes.
Consequently, $\hat d_{i,t}$ is not a calibrated measure of stress duration.
Section~\ref{subsec:beyond_harx} tests one implication of the duration
interpretation directly: if persistence measured how long shocks linger, it
should help forecast the slope of the future variance term structure. It
does not, so the evidence is consistent with uncertainty propagation only
in the weak sense that persistence rises when stress rises.

The distinction from market-state controls is empirical, not absolute.
VIX and MOVE already contain forward-looking volatility information, and
HAR incorporates persistent multi-horizon dynamics. Persistence features
must therefore be assessed against HAR-X rather than credited with the
entire gain of a combined model over HAR. A full-model comparison with
HAR-X measures the joint incremental contribution of the added feature
set; it does not uniquely attribute that gain to cross-sectional averages,
sectoral states, roughness, or individual interactions.

Roughness remains a complementary diagnostic of short-scale irregularity.
Its inclusion alongside memory estimates allows both temporal dimensions
to enter the forecasting exercise, but low estimated Hurst exponents do not
by themselves establish incremental forecast value. The practical
contribution is to evaluate persistence states conditional on established
predictors, not to replace those predictors with a single parameter.

\section{Structured Forecasting with Persistence-Based Features}
\label{sec:ml}
% =============================================================================

If persistence measures contain economically meaningful information, the
next question concerns how such information should be incorporated into
volatility forecasting systems. The approach adopted in this paper treats
persistence measures not as isolated econometric coefficients but as dynamic
state variables describing the temporal structure of financial uncertainty.
Forecasting systems are therefore constructed around economically
interpretable persistence-based features rather than around purely
mechanical lag structures.

The framework developed here occupies an intermediate position between
classical econometric modeling and purely data-driven machine learning.
Traditional parametric forecasting systems provide interpretability and
structural discipline but may fail to capture nonlinear interactions and
regime dependence. Purely flexible machine-learning systems may capture
complex patterns while sacrificing economic transparency and out-of-sample
stability \citep{gu2020empirical}. The objective of the present framework
is therefore not to replace econometric structure with machine learning,
but rather to combine economically interpretable feature construction with
flexible forecasting architectures.

The central hypothesis is that persistence features may contain information
not fully captured by HAR dynamics and VIX/MOVE controls. Whether that
information is useful, and at which horizon, is an empirical question. The
feature design allows shared persistence and its interaction with market
stress to complement conventional predictors without assuming that
persistence directly measures the remaining duration of uncertainty.

The forecasting framework organizes predictors into three groups: HAR
time-aggregation components, market-state indicators (VIX and MOVE), and
persistence-related features and interactions. These groups can contain
overlapping information; their inclusion is not a structural decomposition
of volatility into separately identified economic channels.

Formally, let $y_{t,h}$ denote the future volatility target at forecast
horizon $h$, let $\mathbf X_t$ denote a vector of conventional predictors,
and let $\mathbf Z_t$ denote a vector of persistence-based features. The
forecasting problem may be written as
\begin{equation}
  y_{t,h} \;=\; F\!\bigl(\mathbf X_t,\, \mathbf Z_t\bigr) + u_{t+h},
\end{equation}
where $F(\cdot)$ may represent either a linear or nonlinear forecasting
system. The substantive contribution of the framework lies less in the
specific estimator than in the construction of the persistence feature
space. The feature vector includes several dimensions of persistence
behavior:
\begin{equation}
\begin{split}
\mathbf{Z}_t \; = \; \Bigl( & \hat{d}_t, \, \Delta\hat{d}_t, \, \mathrm{Vol}(\hat{d})_t, \, \mathrm{Trend}(\hat{d})_t, \, H_t, \, \Delta H_t, \, \overline{d}_t, \, \sigma_d^t, \, \overline{d}_{s(i),t}, \\
& \hat{d}_t \!\cdot\! \mathrm{VIX}_t, \, \hat{d}_t \!\cdot\! \mathrm{MOVE}_t \Bigr).
\end{split}
\end{equation}
These variables summarize persistence dynamics at several levels. The
own-stock persistence estimate $ \hat{d}_t$ captures local persistence
behavior. Cross-sectional aggregates $\overline d_t$ and $\sigma_d^t$
summarize system-wide persistence conditions, while sectoral averages
$\overline d_{s(i),t}$ capture industry-specific uncertainty propagation.
Interaction terms such as $ \hat{d}_t\!\cdot\!\mathrm{VIX}_t$ allow the impact of
stress to depend on the persistence state of the market.

The forecasting architecture is organized as a layered ladder of models
designed to isolate the marginal contribution of different persistence
components. The baseline uses HAR components together with lagged-return
controls:
\begin{align}
  \textbf{Model A:}\quad
  &\log\mathrm{RV}^{(d)}_t,\;\log\mathrm{RV}^{(w)}_t,\;\log\mathrm{RV}^{(m)}_t,\;r_{t-1},\;|r_{t-1}|.
  \notag
\end{align}

The first augmentation introduces market-state variables:
\begin{align}
  \textbf{Model }A_1:\quad &A + \bigl(\mathrm{VIX}_t,\;\mathrm{MOVE}_t\bigr).
  \notag
\end{align}
This HAR-X specification provides the market-state benchmark against which
the added persistence feature set must be evaluated.

The second augmentation introduces own-stock persistence and roughness
features:
\begin{align}
  \textbf{Model }A_2:\quad
  &A + \bigl( \hat{d}_t,\;\Delta \hat{d}_t,\;\mathrm{Vol}( \hat{d})_t,\;
  \mathrm{Trend}( \hat{d})_t,\;H_t,\;\Delta H_t\bigr).
  \notag
\end{align}

The third and fourth augmentations introduce cross-sectional and sectoral
persistence features, respectively:
\begin{align}
  \textbf{Model }A_3:\quad &A + \bigl(\overline{d}_t,\;\sigma_d^t\bigr),
  \notag\\
  \textbf{Model }A_4:\quad &A + \overline{d}_{s(i),t}.
  \notag
\end{align}

The fifth augmentation introduces persistence-by-stress interactions:
\begin{align}
  \textbf{Model }A_5:\quad
  &A + \bigl( \hat{d}_t,\;\mathrm{VIX}_t,\;\mathrm{MOVE}_t,\;
   \hat{d}_t\!\cdot\!\mathrm{VIX}_t,\; \hat{d}_t\!\cdot\!\mathrm{MOVE}_t\bigr).
  \notag
\end{align}

The full persistence-augmented forecasting system combines all feature blocks
simultaneously, adding the persistence vector $\mathbf Z_t$ to HAR-X:
\begin{align}
  \textbf{Model C:}\quad &A_1 + \mathbf Z_t.
  \notag
\end{align}

Finally, the same feature space is estimated using several shrinkage and
tree-based procedures, namely the Lasso \citep{tibshirani1996regression},
Ridge regression \citep{hoerl1970ridge}, the Elastic Net
\citep{zou2005regularization}, Random Forests \citep{breiman2001random},
and Gradient Boosting \citep{friedman2001greedy}:
\begin{align}
  \textbf{Model D:}\quad
  &\text{Lasso, Ridge, Elastic Net, Random Forest, Gradient Boosting.}
  \notag
\end{align}

These specifications are parallel augmentations of HAR, not a sequence in
which each model adds to its immediate predecessor. Comparisons of $A_2$,
$A_3$, and $A_4$ with HAR assess the corresponding blocks in isolation.
Because $A_5$ includes VIX and MOVE as well as interactions, its gain over
HAR cannot be attributed to interactions alone. Model~C combines the
feature blocks without duplicating shared predictors; its comparison with
HAR-X assesses their joint incremental contribution conditional on
market-state controls.

Because Models~$A_2$--$A_5$ add persistence blocks to HAR rather than to
HAR-X, their comparison with HAR-X is indirect. Three further
specifications, fixed before the corresponding results were computed, add
one persistence block to HAR-X directly:
\begin{align}
  \textbf{Model }A_1^{cs}:\quad &A_1 + \bigl(\overline{d}_t,\;\sigma_d^t\bigr),
  \notag\\
  \textbf{Model }A_1^{sec}:\quad &A_1 + \overline{d}_{s(i),t},
  \notag\\
  \textbf{Model }A_1^{mod}:\quad &A_1 + \overline{d}_t
  + \overline{d}_t\!\cdot\!\bigl(\log\mathrm{RV}^{(d)}_t,\;
  \log\mathrm{RV}^{(w)}_t,\;\log\mathrm{RV}^{(m)}_t\bigr).
  \notag
\end{align}
The last specification lets the persistence state modulate the weights on
the HAR components, which is the form the duration interpretation of
Section~\ref{sec:structural} implies: if shocks decay more slowly when
persistence is high, the weight on the monthly component should rise with
$\overline d_t$.

The common feature space for Models~C and D also permits comparison of
linear, shrinkage, and tree-based estimators. This comparison evaluates the
implemented algorithms and tuning procedures in this dataset rather than
isolating a universal effect of estimator complexity.

Several implementation safeguards are required because persistence-based
variables are estimated quantities rather than directly observed variables.
Rolling estimation windows are constructed to avoid look-ahead bias and to
preserve the forecasting information set. Feature standardization is
performed using training-sample information only. Hyperparameters are
selected through time-series cross-validation rather than random
cross-sectional shuffling. These precautions are particularly important
because volatility forecasting systems are highly sensitive to temporal
dependence structures.

Forecast evaluation also requires careful treatment because volatility
proxies themselves contain measurement noise. In addition to mean squared
error on the logarithmic realized-volatility scale, the framework therefore
employs the QLIKE loss function discussed by \citet{patton2011volatility},
which is more robust when realized-volatility proxies contain observational
error. For pairwise forecast comparisons, the paper uses the
Diebold--Mariano statistic of \citet{diebold1995comparing} with the
\citet{harvey1997testing} finite-sample correction (HLN), computed on the
cross-sectional mean loss differential per date and using a Newey--West HAC
variance \citep{newey1987simple} with bandwidth $\lceil h/5\rceil-1$. Importantly, inference is
conducted using panel-aware cross-sectional aggregation rather than naive
pooled-cell independence assumptions. This distinction is critical because
ignoring within-date cross-sectional dependence substantially inflates
apparent statistical significance.

Comparisons with HAR-X involve nested models, for which the
Diebold--Mariano statistic is conservative: under the null hypothesis the
larger model's additional coefficients are estimation noise that raises its
out-of-sample loss. We therefore also report the MSPE-adjusted statistic of
\citet{clark2007approximately}, computed on the same cross-sectional mean
differential with the same HAC bandwidth and compared with standard normal
critical values (one-sided), and we adjust the $p$-values of the
pre-specified HAR-X comparisons for multiplicity with the step-down
procedure of \citet{holm1979simple}. A specification is said to add
information beyond HAR-X only if its adjusted Clark--West $p$-value is
below 0.05 and its pooled MSE is lower than that of HAR-X; the
Diebold--Mariano statistic against HAR-X is reported as the stricter
criterion. Differences in Sharpe ratios in Section~\ref{sec:economic} are
tested with the HAC procedure of \citet{ledoit2008robust} and a
studentized circular block bootstrap.

The forecasting structure assesses whether estimated persistence states
provide useful conditional information. Horizon and regime comparisons
describe where that information is most useful; they do not directly test
uncertainty duration or identify the mechanisms behind persistence.

\section{Data and Empirical Design}
\label{sec:data}
% =============================================================================

This section describes the data architecture, volatility construction
procedures, rolling estimation framework, and forecasting protocol used in
the empirical analysis. The central methodological principle is that
persistence and roughness measures must be estimated in a manner consistent
with the temporal structure of financial data while avoiding forward-looking
contamination and preserving comparability across forecasting models.

The empirical framework is designed around three objectives. First, the
data environment must be sufficiently long to capture multiple volatility
regimes, including calm periods, financial crises, and post-crisis
transitions. Second, the volatility proxies must be constructed carefully
enough to distinguish genuine persistence from measurement noise. Third,
the forecasting design must permit rigorous out-of-sample evaluation under
realistic financial forecasting conditions.

\subsection{Sample}

The primary dataset consists of common equities drawn from the S\&P~500
index with sufficiently long daily histories to support rolling estimation
of persistence and roughness measures. Daily open, high, low, close,
volume, and market-capitalization data are obtained from Bloomberg for an
initial universe of 125 securities. After imposing a 70\% data-coverage
requirement over the sample period, the final analysis panel contains
115 stocks observed over 6,136 trading days from November~29, 2001 through
April~21, 2026.\footnote{Data accessed through Bloomberg Terminal on April 21, 2026.}

The 125-security universe is the S\&P~500 membership at the time of the
Bloomberg pull (April 2026). Index membership is not reconstructed
point-in-time, and no stock enters or leaves the panel over the sample, so
the sample is a fixed cross-section of firms that were constituents in
2026 and had sufficiently long histories. Requiring long histories favors
surviving, established firms and excludes shorter-lived or less-completely
observed securities. The results are therefore conditional on the selected
panel rather than representative of all firms that belonged to the index
during 2001--2026, and the coverage threshold alone does not guarantee that
every retained stock is observed on every date. This selection determines
which stocks are forecast; it does not change the information available at
any forecast origin, because every forecast below is per-stock and
walk-forward.

The sample spans several major financial regimes, including the post-2001
recovery period, the credit expansion preceding the global financial
crisis, the global financial crisis itself, the European sovereign-debt
episode, the long low-volatility post-crisis expansion, the COVID shock,
and the post-COVID inflationary environment. The presence of these distinct
regimes is essential because the paper interprets persistence measures as
state-dependent indicators of uncertainty propagation.

In addition to stock-level data, the analysis incorporates several
market-wide variables intended to capture broad financial conditions. These
include the S\&P~500 index, Dow Jones Industrial Average, Nasdaq~100,
Russell~2000, the VIX implied-volatility index, the MOVE Treasury-volatility
index, Treasury-yield series, the 2s/10s yield-curve slope, and
investment-grade and high-yield CDX credit indices. Sector-level
aggregation is implemented using GICS level-1 classifications together
with SPDR sector ETFs.

The resulting dataset provides several important advantages. The long
sample horizon allows persistence measures to be evaluated across multiple
crisis periods and volatility states. The cross-sectional structure permits
construction of aggregate persistence measures and sectoral persistence
indices.

\subsection{Volatility Proxies}

Daily logarithmic returns are computed from adjusted closing prices and
winsorized at the 0.1\% and 99.9\% quantiles of each stock's own history
up to and including the current day (an expanding window, applied once 500
observations are available), so that the clipping thresholds use no future
information. Winsorization reduces the influence of recording errors,
corporate actions, and isolated outlier observations in the predictors; the
portfolio exercise of Section~\ref{sec:economic} uses raw returns. Because volatility measurement plays a
central role in persistence estimation, the choice of volatility proxy is
particularly important.

The primary volatility measure used throughout the paper is the range-based
variance estimator of \citet{parkinson1980extreme},
\begin{equation}
  \mathrm{RVPK}_t \;=\; \frac{(\ln H_t - \ln L_t)^2}{4\ln 2}.
  \label{eq:parkinson}
\end{equation}
In Equation~\eqref{eq:parkinson}, $H_t$ and $L_t$ denote the daily high
and low prices; elsewhere, $H$ denotes the Hurst exponent. Unlike squared
close-to-close returns, the Parkinson estimator exploits intraday price
range information and therefore provides a more efficient volatility proxy
under continuous-diffusion assumptions. The Parkinson measure offers several
advantages for the present application. First, it can be constructed
over the long daily sample without requiring transaction-level data. Second,
it substantially reduces measurement noise relative to squared returns.
Third, the estimator captures volatility clustering particularly clearly,
which is important for persistence estimation.

For robustness, the forecasting comparison in Section~\ref{sec:results}
is also repeated with the logarithm of future mean squared returns as the
target, a noisier close-to-close volatility proxy.

For forecasting purposes, the volatility target for stock $i$ at horizon
$h$ is defined as the logarithm of the future average Parkinson variance,
\begin{equation}
  y_{i,t,h} \;=\; \log\!\Bigl(\frac{1}{h}\!\sum_{j=1}^{h}
  \mathrm{RVPK}_{i,t+j}\Bigr).
\end{equation}
Three forecast horizons are considered, $h\in\{1,5,22\}$, corresponding
approximately to daily, weekly, and monthly horizons. This design assesses
whether the incremental contribution of persistence varies with the
forecast horizon rather than assuming a uniform benefit.

\subsection{Preliminary Diagnostics}

Before proceeding to rolling persistence estimation and forecasting
analysis, it is important to examine several basic empirical properties of
the data. The preliminary diagnostics illustrate the stylized facts motivating the
paper: volatility clustering, heavy tails, cross-sectional comovement, and
persistent conditional heteroskedasticity.

Figure~\ref{fig:data_overview} presents four preliminary diagnostics for a
representative stock, together with the VIX index. Panel~(a) displays
daily logarithmic returns for AAPL, while panel~(b) reports the
corresponding Parkinson range-based variance proxy. The contrast between
the two series immediately reveals one of the classical stylized facts of
financial econometrics. Returns themselves fluctuate around zero with
little visually detectable serial dependence, whereas volatility measures
display pronounced clustering. Large volatility episodes tend to be
followed by additional large volatility episodes, while calm periods tend
to persist as well.

The recession-shaded regions corresponding to the global financial crisis
and COVID are especially informative. During these periods, the Parkinson
variance proxy rises sharply and remains elevated for extended intervals
rather than reverting immediately to calm-state levels. This behavior
motivates studying dependence alongside volatility magnitude, without
equating a persistence estimate with a direct measure of stress duration.

Panel~(c) of Figure~\ref{fig:data_overview} compares autocorrelation
functions for raw returns, squared returns, and Parkinson realized
variance. Raw returns exhibit little serial dependence at the displayed
lags, consistent with
the approximate unpredictability of returns documented throughout the
financial-econometrics literature. By contrast, both squared returns and
the Parkinson variance proxy display autocorrelations that decay slowly and
remain positive beyond the first few lags. The Parkinson
autocorrelations generally lie above those for squared returns,
suggesting that the range-based estimator provides a less noisy volatility
proxy.

This distinction is important because persistence estimation is highly
sensitive to measurement error. Squared returns contain substantial noise
arising from isolated jumps and market microstructure effects. By exploiting
intraday high--low information, the Parkinson estimator substantially
improves the signal-to-noise ratio of volatility measurement.

Panel~(d) presents the VIX time series over the full sample period. The
VIX displays several dramatic spikes corresponding to major stress
episodes, particularly the global financial crisis and COVID. These broad
market stress indicators later play a central role in the
persistence-by-stress interaction framework developed in
Section~\ref{sec:ml}.

\subsection{Rolling Estimation Design}

Persistence and roughness measures are estimated within rolling windows so
that they behave as dynamic state variables rather than fixed full-sample
constants. This distinction is essential for the interpretation developed
throughout the paper. If persistence reflects evolving uncertainty
conditions, information diffusion, and regime-dependent stress propagation,
then persistence measures themselves must be allowed to vary over time.

For each stock $i$ and each end date $t$, the fractional integration
parameter $ \hat{d}_{i,t}$ is estimated using a rolling 750-trading-day
window. Both the Geweke--Porter-Hudak log-periodogram estimator and the
local-Whittle semiparametric estimator are employed using bandwidth
$m = \lfloor T^{0.65}\rfloor$. The rolling estimates are updated every
five trading days, producing approximately weekly time series of
$ \hat{d}_{i,t}^{GPH}$, $ \hat{d}_{i,t}^{LW}$, and $H_{i,t}$, where $H_{i,t}$
denotes the rolling Hurst exponent estimated from logarithmic Parkinson
range-based variance.

Weekly updating balances responsiveness to market conditions against
short-term estimation variation. It provides an intermediate frequency
between daily updates and the greater smoothing associated with monthly
updates.

The 750-day estimation window is similarly motivated by the tradeoff
between statistical precision and state sensitivity. Persistence estimation
requires relatively long windows because long-memory parameters are
fundamentally low-frequency objects. Very short windows generate unstable
estimates with large sampling variance. Extremely long windows, however,
blur structural regime changes and reduce responsiveness to evolving market
conditions. The selected window length therefore balances these competing
considerations.

The rolling framework transforms persistence from a static full-sample
coefficient into a genuinely time-varying market-state variable. This
transformation is central to the economic interpretation of the paper.
Under the rolling design, persistence measures may rise during prolonged
stress episodes and decline during calm periods, allowing analysis
of state-dependent persistence, not direct measurement of stress duration.

These rolling estimates provide the forecasting features introduced in
Section~\ref{sec:ml}, as well as additional descriptive diagnostics. The
resulting variables fall into several categories. The first category consists of own-stock
persistence dynamics,
\begin{equation}
  \hat{d}_{i,t},\, \Delta \hat{d}_{i,t},\;
  \mathrm{Vol}( \hat{d})_{i,t},\, \mathrm{Trend}( \hat{d})_{i,t},\,  H_{i,t},\, \Delta H_{i,t},
\end{equation}

summarizing the level, evolution, variability, and trend structure of
persistence and roughness at the individual-stock level. The second
category consists of cross-sectional persistence aggregates,
\begin{equation}
  \overline{d}_t,\;\sigma_d^t,\;\mathrm{Skew}_t(d),\;\mathrm{Kurt}_t(d),
\end{equation}
which summarize system-wide persistence conditions. The cross-sectional
mean $\overline d_t$ is especially important because the paper interprets
it as a summary of shared volatility dependence in
financial markets. The third category consists of sectoral persistence
measures, $\overline d_{s(i),t}$, where the average is computed across
stocks belonging to the same GICS sector as stock $i$, intended to capture
industry-specific stress propagation and heterogeneous information environments.


Additional descriptive variables include threshold indicators $\mathbf 1_{\{ \hat{d}_{i,t}
> \tau\}}$ together with interaction terms involving VIX, MOVE, and
liquidity conditions. The forecasting models use the predictors explicitly
listed in Section~\ref{sec:ml}; the broader descriptive inventory should
not be interpreted as additional regressors in every model.

Table~\ref{tab:features} reports pooled descriptive statistics for the
resulting feature set. Several patterns are immediately noteworthy. First,
the GPH and local-Whittle rolling estimates are close on average (pooled
means of 0.346 and 0.336), although the GPH estimates are somewhat more
dispersed. Second, the reported Hurst
estimates are well below $\frac{1}{2}$, consistent with roughness in the
observed volatility proxy. Third, the cross-sectional mean persistence measure varies
substantially over time, supporting its interpretation as a dynamic state
variable rather than a stable structural constant.

\input{tables/table4_features.tex}
The rolling estimates are not uniformly within the covariance-stationary
fractional range: the reported 99th percentiles are 0.8308 (GPH) and 0.8278
(local-Whittle), above $d=\frac{1}{2}$, and roughly one fifth of the rolling
GPH estimates exceed that threshold. A mean estimate below the threshold does
not establish stationarity in every window. We therefore use rolling estimates
as reduced-form dependence features and do not interpret large values as
verified parameters of a stationary fractional process.

The persistence-by-VIX interaction is larger during crisis periods. Because
it is a product of its inputs, this descriptive pattern alone does not
establish an amplification mechanism.

Rolling persistence features are constructed from observations available
through date $t$, preserving the forecasting information set. The
walk-forward protocol is described below.

The rolling framework permits comparison of estimated dependence across
market states. Because long windows smooth and overlap, movements in these
estimates should not be read as dates of shock arrival or measurements of
how quickly a particular disturbance is resolved.

Moreover, because the rolling framework generates persistence measures for
each stock, sector, and date simultaneously, it becomes possible to study
not only average persistence but also the cross-sectional dispersion and
heterogeneity of persistence conditions throughout the financial system.
The resulting persistence-state panel therefore forms the core empirical
object underlying the forecasting analysis developed in the next section.
 

 

\subsection{Out-of-Sample Forecasting Protocol}

The forecast evaluation is strictly walk-forward and point-in-time. We use
the first 40\% of the sample dates (sample dates 1 through 431, ending in
June 2013) as the initial training window; thereafter, we produce forecasts
at every subsequent sample date. At each origin $t$ the training sample
contains only rows whose target window ends on or before the origin day,
that is, rows $j$ with $\mathrm{pos}_j + h \le \mathrm{pos}_t$ in trading
days. With a five-day sampling stride this excludes no rows at $h=1$ or
$h=5$ (the weekly target of the preceding row ends exactly on the origin
day) and the four most recent rows at $h=22$, whose monthly windows would
otherwise extend up to 17 trading days past the origin. All predictors
dated $t$ are known at the close of day $t$: the HAR components and the
rolling persistence and roughness estimates end on day $t-1$, the last
completed observation before the origin, while VIX and MOVE are the closes
on day $t$. For the linear specifications we refit by ordinary least
squares at every step (expanding window). For Model~$D$ we refit every
twenty sample steps to keep the computational burden tractable for the
tree-based estimators, applying the same exclusion rule at each refit;
results are not sensitive to this stride. All feature standardization,
hyperparameter selection, and model selection are performed on
training-window information only. Both the realized target and the
forecast are recorded as $(T_{\mathrm{eval}}\!\times\!N)$ panels, which are
then used to calculate forecast losses, the test statistics, and the regime
and sector comparisons in Section~\ref{sec:results}.

\section{Numerical Analysis and Forecasting Results}
\label{sec:results}
% =============================================================================

This section presents the empirical results of the paper. The analysis
proceeds in several stages. First, we examine full-sample estimates of
long-memory and roughness parameters across the cross-section of equities.
Second, we study the dynamics of rolling persistence measures across market
regimes. Third, we evaluate the forecasting performance of the layered
forecasting framework developed in Section~\ref{sec:ml}. Finally, we
investigate robustness across sectors, volatility states, liquidity
conditions, and alternative estimation procedures.

The central empirical question is whether persistence measures behave like
economically meaningful state variables rather than purely statistical
nuisance parameters. Under the interpretation developed earlier, persistence
measures should rise during sustained stress episodes, co-move with broad
uncertainty indicators, and contribute forecasting information particularly
during periods in which uncertainty resolves slowly.

\subsection{Long-Memory and Roughness Estimates}

We begin with full-sample cross-sectional estimates of persistence and
roughness. The results indicate persistent dependence and low Hurst estimates in
the observed volatility proxies across the equity panel.

The first important finding is that raw returns themselves display
essentially no evidence of long memory. The cross-sectional mean GPH
estimate for returns is approximately $ \hat{d}^{GPH}_{\mathrm{returns}}
= -0.022$, and only 15\% of stocks exhibit statistical significance at
the 5\% level. This result is consistent with the approximate
unpredictability of financial returns documented throughout the empirical
asset-pricing literature.

The volatility proxies show a different pattern.
For the Parkinson range-based variance proxy, the cross-sectional mean GPH
estimate equals approximately $ \hat{d}^{GPH}_{\mathrm{RV}} = 0.451$, while
the corresponding local-Whittle estimate equals approximately
$ \hat{d}^{LW}_{\mathrm{RV}} = 0.440$. The two semiparametric estimators
therefore agree closely, both on average and sector by sector
(Table~\ref{tab:lrd_estimates}). Moreover, 99\% (GPH) and 100\%
(local-Whittle) of stocks exhibit statistically significant persistence
estimates. These results provide strong evidence that
long-memory behavior is a broad characteristic of volatility dynamics
rather than an isolated feature of a few securities.

The roughness diagnostics provide complementary evidence. The cross-sectional
mean Hurst exponent estimated from logarithmic Parkinson range-based variance
equals approximately $H = 0.063$, with every stock in the panel satisfying
$H < \frac{1}{2}$. This result is consistent with local irregularity in the observed proxy;
it does not by itself identify the path regularity of latent volatility. The cross-sectional
dispersion of Hurst estimates is small, indicating similar proxy-based
roughness estimates across sectors and firms.

Table~\ref{tab:lrd_estimates} reports these estimates separately by GICS
sector. Financials and Real Estate have relatively large persistence
estimates, consistent with their exposure to systemic stress propagation,
leverage conditions, and liquidity cycles. Utilities and Consumer Staples
display somewhat smaller persistence estimates, although long-memory
behavior remains substantial throughout all sectors. An additional feature
of the results is the limited dispersion of persistence estimates across
the cross-section: the standard deviation of persistence estimates across
stocks remains relatively modest, indicating that long-memory volatility
behavior is fundamentally a panel-wide phenomenon rather than the
consequence of a few extreme outliers.

Figure~\ref{fig:lrd_estimates} reports four time-series and cross-sectional
views of the rolling estimation. Panel~(a) reports the distribution of
full-sample GPH estimates across all 115 stocks. The distribution is
concentrated between 0.30 and 0.60 (84\% of stocks) around the mean of 0.451;
about 30\% of the full-sample estimates lie above $\frac{1}{2}$, the boundary
of the covariance-stationary range, which is one reason the paper treats
$\hat d$ as a reduced-form dependence measure rather than a verified
parameter of a stationary fractional process.

Panel~(b) reports rolling $ \hat{d}^{GPH}$ for five representative stocks
(AAPL, JPM, XOM, JNJ, PG). The most striking feature is the broad upward
shift in persistence during major crisis episodes, particularly during the
global financial crisis and COVID.

Panel~(c) presents the aggregate state central to the forecasting
framework: the cross-sectional mean persistence state $\overline d_t =
N^{-1}\sum_i  \hat{d}_{i,t}$, together with a one-standard-deviation band.
The dynamics of this aggregate persistence state are highly informative.
During the calm 2013--2014 period, the cross-sectional mean persistence
measure is approximately $\overline d_t \approx 0.31$. During
the global financial crisis (2008-Q3 through 2009-Q4), the cross-sectional
mean rises to approximately $\overline d_t \approx 0.50$, an increase of
approximately 62\%. During COVID (March--December 2020), the persistence
state rises even further to approximately $\overline d_t \approx 0.57$, an
increase of approximately 86\% relative to the calm-state baseline.

These increases document an association between estimated persistence and
major stress episodes. They are consistent with prolonged uncertainty
propagation, but do not measure the time needed to resolve uncertainty or
distinguish that interpretation from regime-dependent changes in volatility.

Panel~(d) of Figure~\ref{fig:lrd_estimates} examines the relationship
between the cross-sectional persistence state and the VIX. The estimated
contemporaneous correlation equals approximately
$\rho(\overline d_t, \text{VIX}_t) = 0.50$. This correlation shows that persistence and VIX are related but not
identical. It does not establish conditional forecast value, a leading
relationship, or the expected duration of uncertainty. The benchmark and
lead--lag comparisons below assess predictive usefulness and temporal
association, not duration.



Taken together, these findings motivate the reduced-form interpretation of
Section~\ref{sec:structural}: persistence varies with market conditions and
may summarize shared dependence relevant to forecasting. The next
subsection tests its predictive usefulness beyond HAR and HAR-X rather than
assuming that its association with stress establishes that usefulness.

\subsection{Out-of-Sample Forecast Comparison}

Table~\ref{tab:model_comparison} compares the HAR core (Model~A), its
parallel augmentations ($A_1$--$A_5$), the full feature specification (C),
and alternative estimators (D) at horizons $h\in\{1,5,22\}$. Performance is
measured by mean squared error on the log Parkinson-variance target and
QLIKE on the variance scale. Positive percentage improvements denote lower
MSE than HAR. The reported panel-aware HLN-corrected Diebold--Mariano
statistics also use HAR as the reference; they do not test Model~C against
HAR-X.

\input{tables/table5_model_comparison.tex}

The HAR-X benchmark is essential for interpreting the results. Adding VIX
and MOVE alone ($A_1$) reduces MSE relative to HAR by 5.10\%, 7.73\%, and
2.82\% at the daily, weekly, and monthly horizons. The reported tests reject
equal predictive accuracy against HAR at the daily and weekly horizons
($t = 5.34$ and $5.31$) but not at the monthly horizon ($t = 1.49$).
Market-state variables therefore account for a substantial part of the
improvement available over volatility lags alone at short horizons.

Adding only own-stock persistence and roughness ($A_2$) does not improve
MSE: the corresponding changes relative to HAR are $-0.54\%$, $-0.03\%$,
and $-1.89\%$. The daily and monthly deteriorations are statistically
significant under the reported test. This result shows that estimating individual persistence does not
automatically provide information beyond the multi-horizon HAR components.

Cross-sectional aggregates alone ($A_3$) yield changes of $-0.10\%$,
$+0.37\%$, and $+0.05\%$, while sectoral persistence alone ($A_4$) yields
$-0.05\%$, $+0.21\%$, and $-0.16\%$. None of these changes is statistically
significant at the reported levels. These blocks are therefore potential components of a joint state
representation, not independently established sources of forecast gains.

Model~$A_5$, which includes market controls as well as persistence--stress
interactions, improves on HAR by 4.91\%, 7.63\%, and 0.39\%. Each of these
MSE gains is smaller than the corresponding HAR-X gain. Its performance
cannot be read as evidence that interactions alone outperform market-state
controls, although its QLIKE values are slightly lower than those of HAR-X
at the daily and weekly horizons.

The full persistence-augmented specification (C) reduces MSE relative to
HAR by 4.55\%, 8.25\%, and $-0.62\%$. Its reported HLN statistics are 3.15,
3.88, and $-0.21$, respectively, indicating significance against HAR at
the 1\% level at the daily and weekly horizons and no difference at the
monthly horizon. Relative to HAR-X the pattern is stark. Model~C has higher
daily MSE (0.6382 versus 0.6345), slightly lower weekly MSE (0.3335 versus
0.3353), and higher monthly MSE (0.2726 versus 0.2633). Expressed on the
common HAR-relative improvement scale, these differences are $-0.55$,
$+0.52$, and $-3.44$ percentage points. Direct significance tests of C and
of every other specification against HAR-X are reported in
Section~\ref{subsec:beyond_harx}.

Thus, market-state variables account for essentially all of the gain over
HAR, and at the monthly horizon the joint persistence set is worse than
HAR-X. Under QLIKE the full model is marginally below HAR-X at the daily
and weekly horizons and above it at the monthly horizon, so the ranking is
not an artifact of the loss function. This evidence supports persistence at
most as a complement to established predictors, not as a source of
additional accuracy.

\subsection{Incremental Accuracy Relative to HAR-X}
\label{subsec:beyond_harx}

\input{tables/table11_harx_tests.tex}

Table~\ref{tab:harx_tests} tests every specification directly against
HAR-X. Three facts stand out. First, by the Diebold--Mariano criterion
Model~C is never significantly more accurate than HAR-X: the HLN statistics
are $-0.74$, $+0.48$, and $-2.44$ at the daily, weekly, and monthly
horizons. Second, the Clark--West statistic rejects the nested null for
Model~C at every horizon ($t = 6.1$, $7.7$, $6.3$) and for the pre-specified
$A_1^{cs}$ and $A_1^{mod}$ specifications at the daily and weekly horizons
(Holm-adjusted $p \le 0.009$): the persistence regressors have non-zero
population coefficients. Third, that information does not survive
estimation. None of the three HAR-X-nesting specifications lowers pooled
MSE relative to HAR-X at any horizon (the changes range from $-0.15\%$ to
$-2.25\%$), and Model~C does so only at the weekly horizon and only by
$0.57\%$. Under the decision rule stated in Section~\ref{sec:ml}, Model~C at
$h=5$ is the single case that qualifies, and its Diebold--Mariano statistic
against HAR-X is $+0.48$. The $A_1^{mod}$ specification, which lets the
persistence state modulate the HAR weights as the duration interpretation
implies, is worse than HAR-X at all three horizons.

\input{tables/table13_candidates.tex}

The combination of Clark--West rejections and flat or negative MSE
differences points to estimation noise: each per-stock regression estimates
between three and thirteen additional coefficients on a few hundred weekly
observations. Table~\ref{tab:candidates} reports three designs, fixed in
advance, that address this and test the economic interpretation directly.
Pooled estimation with stock fixed effects (Panel~A) recovers a gain of
$0.20\%$ for the full persistence set over pooled HAR-X at the daily
horizon (Clark--West Holm-adjusted $p = 0.033$, Diebold--Mariano
$t = 0.36$), $0.19\%$ at the weekly horizon ($p = 0.058$), and a loss at
the monthly horizon; pooling does not improve HAR-X itself. The
estimation-noise explanation is therefore correct, but the recoverable
information is economically negligible. Panel~B replaces the level target
with the log ratio of monthly to weekly future variance, the quantity that
a duration measure should predict: adding the cross-sectional persistence
state to HAR-X changes MSE by $-0.13\%$, and the full set by $-1.80\%$
(Diebold--Mariano $t = -3.12$). Panel~C forecasts the cross-sectional mean
of the stock targets as a single market series: the persistence state
lowers accuracy relative to market HAR-X at every horizon ($-1.2\%$,
$-1.9\%$, $-6.7\%$). To a close approximation, the persistence state's
forecasting content is its co-movement with the VIX.

\subsection{Descriptive Coefficient Diagnostics}

Table~\ref{tab:importance} reports standardized Lasso coefficients from one
pooled fit per horizon using all out-of-sample evaluation dates. These are
retrospective descriptive fits, not the walk-forward forecasts evaluated
in Table~\ref{tab:model_comparison}. They describe conditional associations
within the feature set rather than providing independent out-of-sample
validation or a causal decomposition of forecast gains.

\input{tables/table6_feature_importance.tex}

The HAR components remain prominent. The monthly component has the largest
coefficient at each horizon, rising from 0.3540 at $h=1$ to 0.4499 at
$h=22$. VIX also enters positively, with a coefficient declining from
0.2784 to 0.1132. These patterns reinforce the importance of established
volatility dynamics and market-state information.

The own-stock memory coefficient is positive, whereas cross-sectional mean
persistence and the own-stock persistence--VIX interaction enter negatively. The
persistence--VIX coefficient is substantial in magnitude, ranging from
$-0.1453$ to $-0.1151$. Sectoral persistence coefficients are positive but
small. These signs describe conditional associations in a model containing
correlated predictors and interactions; they do not imply that higher
persistence necessarily raises forecast volatility or prolongs stress.

Standardization aids comparison of coefficient magnitudes, but Lasso
selection does not supply significance tests. Correlated inputs can also
redistribute coefficients across related predictors. Consequently, retained
aggregate features are not evidence that each block independently improves
forecasting, and retention of own-stock memory does not contradict the weak
performance of $A_2$. The forecast comparisons, rather than coefficient
magnitudes alone, establish the practical distinction between individual
persistence and the joint state representation.

\subsection{Regime Split}

Table~\ref{tab:regime_split} reports MSE improvements relative to HAR by
market regime and horizon. It distinguishes two questions: whether the
combined forecasting system is useful during stress, and whether its
persistence features add accuracy beyond HAR-X within those same states.
The reported regime comparisons are descriptive and do not include
regime-specific tests of C against HAR-X.

\input{tables/table7_subsamples.tex}

During high-VIX states, Model~C improves on HAR by 11.83\%, 17.93\%, and
1.07\% at the daily, weekly, and monthly horizons, compared with HAR-X gains
of 11.44\%, 16.03\%, and 5.58\%. The full model therefore adds 0.39 and

percentage points at the monthly horizon on the HAR-relative improvement

COVID is starker. Model~C improves on HAR by 6.87\%, 18.92\%, and 7.24\%,

weekly 18.92\% gain is relative to HAR, not HAR-X, and is HAR-X's gain.

Gains over HAR also occur in calm states. At the weekly horizon, Model~C
improves by 6.59\% in the low-VIX regime, versus 5.60\% for HAR-X. The
regime pattern therefore does not support the idea that persistence pays

\subsection{Robustness}

The final step in the empirical analysis is to determine whether the
forecasting improvements remain stable across alternative estimation
procedures, rolling-window choices, volatility proxies, liquidity
conditions, and benchmark specifications.

Table~\ref{tab:robustness} summarizes a broad robustness battery for the
headline comparison between the full persistence-augmented model and the HAR benchmark
at the weekly forecasting horizon. The reported variants retain positive gains against HAR, subject to the
benchmark and measurement qualifications discussed below.
\input{tables/table9_robustness.tex}
Replacing the GPH persistence estimator throughout the feature set with
the local-Whittle estimator produces forecasting improvements almost
identical to the headline specification: $+8.21\%$ versus the baseline
$+8.25\%$. This result is important because it demonstrates that the
forecasting gains are not driven by the particular semiparametric estimator
used to construct the persistence variables.

The rolling-window robustness checks produce similarly stable results.
Using a shorter 500-day rolling estimation window yields approximately
$+9.13\%$ forecast improvement, while using a longer 1000-day window
yields approximately $+8.68\%$. Both forecasting gains remain statistically
significant. These findings indicate that the forecasting gains are not
artifacts of a particular rolling-window length. Persistence-based
forecasting variables remain informative across substantially different
temporal smoothing structures.

The volatility-proxy robustness checks are also revealing. Replacing the
Parkinson realized-variance target with the logarithm of future mean
squared returns, a substantially noisier volatility proxy, reduces
the magnitude of the forecasting gains but still produces statistically
significant improvement: $+4.19\%$. This result suggests that the
persistence-based forecasting gains are not specific to the Parkinson
volatility estimator itself. The smaller gain is consistent with
sensitivity to the volatility proxy, but the comparison does not isolate
measurement noise as its cause.

Liquidity splits show gains over HAR in both groups: 8.95\% in the
low-liquidity half and 7.52\% in the high-liquidity half. The larger gain in
the former is consistent with a liquidity-related interpretation, but the
split does not identify a liquidity mechanism. These groups use a static
full-sample liquidity classification and are retrospective diagnostics,
not real-time trading rules.

The GARCH(1,1) benchmark row warrants comment. A conventional GARCH(1,1)
model estimated directly on returns performs substantially worse than the
HAR benchmark when evaluated against the Parkinson range-based variance
target: $-95.9\%$ relative performance under mean squared error loss.
Under QLIKE, the reported HLN DM statistic is $-2.96$. This statistic
is not directly comparable to a percentage MSE change, since the loss
functions use different scales. Comparisons between returns-based
conditional variance models and range-based proxies involve substantial scale mismatch
and measurement differences. The paper therefore emphasizes that the
forecasting comparison should primarily be interpreted within the
realized-volatility forecasting environment rather than as a definitive
rejection of all conditional-variance models.

Finally, the robustness analysis again illustrates the importance of
correct statistical inference. The naive pooled-cell Diebold--Mariano
statistic ignoring cross-sectional dependence equals approximately
$+23.99$, whereas the properly panel-aware HLN-corrected statistic equals
approximately $+3.88$. This difference illustrates how
apparent statistical significance may be inflated when within-date
cross-sectional dependence is ignored.

Taken together, the robustness checks show that the combined model's
weekly gains against HAR persist under the reported estimator, window,
proxy, and liquidity variations. They compare C with HAR; the comparison
that matters, C against HAR-X, is in Section~\ref{subsec:beyond_harx} and
is not favorable. The checks also do not resolve sample selection or
structural-break identification.

\section{Economic Interpretation and Implications}
\label{sec:economic}
% =============================================================================

The forecasting evidence does not support using persistence states as
additional predictors once implied-volatility indices are available; it
does support treating the cross-sectional persistence state as a
descriptive indicator of market conditions. The portfolio exercise applies
the existing forecasts and tests whether the small forecast differences
translate into risk-adjusted performance; the broader implications for
stress monitoring remain potential uses rather than separately validated
results.

\subsection{Volatility-Managed Portfolios}
\label{subsec:volmanaged}

Following \citet{moreira2017volatility}, the paper constructs equity
portfolios that scale exposure inversely with forecast variance. For stock
$i$ at date $t$, the managed five-day return is
\begin{equation}
  f_{m,i,t} \;=\; \frac{c_{m,i}}{\hat{\sigma}^2_{m,i,t}}\,r_{i,t\to t+5},
\end{equation}
where $\hat\sigma^2_{m,i,t}=\exp(\hat y_{m,i,t})$ is the exponentiated
log-variance forecast and $c_{m,i,t}$ is a stock- and model-specific
normalization constant that equates the variance of the managed and
unmanaged stock returns over the expanding window of weeks strictly before
$t$, after a 52-week warm-up, so that the strategy is implementable in real
time; the \citet{moreira2017volatility} constant computed over the full
evaluation sample is reported as a robustness row. Portfolio returns use
raw (unwinsorized) log returns. The Parkinson proxy is the forecast
target; this construction should not be interpreted as directly forecasting
the conditional variance of portfolio returns.

Table~\ref{tab:volmanaged} shows similar Sharpe ratios over the 593-week
evaluation sample that follows the normalization warm-up: 0.61 for the
unmanaged portfolio, 0.63 for HAR-X management, and 0.66 for Model~C
management. Model~C has a smaller maximum drawdown than both comparators
($-17.4\%$ versus $-18.2\%$ and $-31.9\%$) and a marginally higher
certainty-equivalent return (4.19\% versus 3.93\% and 3.34\%). The
full-sample-constant construction of \citet{moreira2017volatility} gives
a lower Sharpe ratio (0.59) for the same forecasts, so the real-time
version does not flatter the strategy.

During COVID, Model~C management produces a Sharpe ratio of 1.41, compared
with 1.11 for HAR-X management and 0.50 for the unmanaged portfolio. Its
certainty-equivalent return is 12.04\%, versus 8.57\% and $-11.12\%$,
respectively. Its COVID maximum drawdown is larger than that of HAR-X
management in absolute magnitude (4.73\% versus 4.04\%). In high-VIX states,
the Sharpe ratios for Model~C and HAR-X management are 0.78 and 0.71; in
low-VIX states, they are 1.15 and 1.14.

Table~\ref{tab:sharpe_tests} asks whether any of these differences is
distinguishable from zero. None is. The annualized Sharpe difference
between Model~C and HAR-X management is $+0.03$ over the full sample
(Ledoit--Wolf HAC $p = 0.31$, bootstrap $p = 0.29$), $+0.07$ in high-VIX

These portfolio comparisons need not reproduce the MSE ranking. During
COVID at $h=5$, HAR-X has lower forecast MSE than Model~C, yet the
Model~C-managed portfolio has the higher Sharpe ratio. Forecast loss and
portfolio performance evaluate different objects: inverse-variance weights
and realized returns determine the latter. The portfolio result is an
application-level comparison, not proof that persistence improves COVID
weekly forecast accuracy or uniquely causes the Sharpe difference.

\subsection{Implications for Risk Monitoring}

The cross-sectional persistence state rises during the global financial
crisis and COVID and has a contemporaneous correlation of approximately
0.50 with VIX. These full-sample diagnostics motivate monitoring dependence
alongside volatility levels. They do not establish that persistence predicts
the onset of stress or the time until conditions normalize. The lead--lag
correlations and the smoothing induced by rolling estimation do not
establish a directional forecasting relationship.

For risk management, the immediate implication is that a persistence state
is a useful monitoring variable but not a forecasting input once
implied-volatility indices are available. It may still motivate scenarios
involving more persistent volatility or sectorally concentrated
dependence. Applications to crisis-duration
forecasting, systemic early warning, multivariate risk, and volatility
trading remain extensions requiring their own evaluation. The current
results do not validate those uses.

\subsection{Implications for Forecasting Design}

The comparison across estimators favors a structured linear use of the
feature set over the evaluated tree-based alternatives. The relevant
methodological lesson is conditional: interpretable feature construction
can be useful without a highly flexible estimator in this panel and
forecasting protocol. It is not a general claim that nonlinear methods
cannot forecast volatility, nor does the comparison alone establish
whether sample size, tuning, or estimation instability explains their
performance. HAR-X remains a strong benchmark throughout.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}
% =============================================================================

This paper evaluates volatility persistence as a financial state variable
rather than solely as a stock-specific fractional parameter. Across 115
equities drawn from the S\&P~500 over November 2001--April 2026, the
volatility proxies display persistent dependence and low estimated Hurst
exponents. The cross-sectional mean GPH and local-Whittle estimates are
0.451 and 0.440, respectively, and the mean Hurst estimate is approximately
0.063. These diagnostics are consistent with persistence at longer scales
and local irregularity, without identifying a unique latent volatility
process or establishing stationarity in every rolling window.

The main finding is that the forecasting content of this state is already
contained in the market-state controls. The own-stock persistence and
roughness block adds nothing to HAR. The shared persistence state,
combined with VIX and MOVE, improves on HAR by 4.5\% and 8.3\% at the
daily and weekly horizons, but HAR-X alone achieves 5.1\% and 7.7\%, and
at the monthly horizon neither the full model nor any persistence block
improves on HAR once the training window is restricted to information
available at the forecast origin.

Tested directly, the full model is never significantly more accurate than
HAR-X (Diebold--Mariano statistics of $-0.74$, $+0.48$, and $-2.44$).
Clark--West tests show that the persistence regressors do carry
population information, but three specifications fixed in advance that add
persistence to HAR-X directly, including one in which persistence
modulates the HAR weights, all fail to lower forecast error. Pooled
estimation across stocks recovers a gain of two tenths of one percent at
the daily horizon, the persistence state does not forecast the slope of the
future variance term structure, and it adds nothing to VIX and MOVE at
the market level. The duration interpretation that motivates persistence
as a state variable is therefore not supported as a forecastable quantity.

The same holds across regimes and sectors. The full model's large gains
over HAR in stressed periods are HAR-X's gains; it trails HAR-X at every
horizon during COVID; and its weekly sectoral advantages are not
accompanied by significant differences from HAR-X.

The volatility-managed portfolio exercise, with real-time normalization,
gives Model~C management a COVID Sharpe ratio of 1.41 versus 1.11 for
HAR-X management and 0.66 versus 0.63 over the full sample; neither
difference is statistically significant ($p = 0.15$ and $0.31$). The
estimator comparisons favor the structured linear specification over the
evaluated tree-based procedures within this setting, not linear methods in
general.

Several limitations define the scope of the findings. The panel is the
S\&P~500 membership of April 2026 and is subject to survivorship and
selection effects; range-based volatility measurement is not equivalent to
observing latent volatility; and rolling estimates can reflect regime
changes as well as fractional dependence, with some values outside the
stationary range. The persistence state is a coherent descriptive indicator
of market stress, but no causal mechanism is identified, and the VIX
lead--lag diagnostic documents an association, not a validated leading
indicator.

Future work could assess point-in-time constituent panels, richer
high-frequency measurements, other asset classes, and settings without a
liquid implied-volatility index, where the information that persistence
shares with the VIX would have to come from persistence itself. The present
contribution is the characterization: a shared persistence state describes
market conditions well, and its forecasting content for stock-level
variance is already priced in implied volatility.
